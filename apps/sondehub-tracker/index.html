what am I looking at? (tap for the 60-second version)

Radiosondes are real weather instruments: small battery-powered sensor boxes carried up by hydrogen or helium balloons, released around the world (typically twice a day per station) to measure temperature, humidity, pressure and winds through the whole atmosphere — the raw material of every weather forecast. As the balloon climbs the air thins and the balloon swells until it bursts, usually somewhere around 20–35 km up.

Where this data comes from: SondeHub (sondehub.org — named here, not linked, per this site's no-external-links rule) is an open-source, volunteer-run network: radio hobbyists around the world run receiver stations that pick up each balloon's telemetry signal and share it publicly, and the tracking software and infrastructure are community-built open source (it grew out of the amateur-radio high-altitude-balloon community). Every position you see here was heard out of the air by a volunteer's antenna. This page is an unofficial client of their documented public API, with thanks.

After burst, the instrument falls back to Earth — and that's why they descend by parachute: a small chute (or the balloon's remains acting as one) slows the fall so a few hundred grams of electronics arrives gently instead of dangerously, protecting people and property below. The descent you see in the telemetry here is that parachute ride. Hobbyists listen to the radio signal, predict the landing, and often recover the instruments — always respecting private property and staying clear of power lines and water.

Reusing a recovered RS41 (the short version): the Vaisala RS41 — the most common sonde you'll see here — leads a double life after recovery. As a factory sensor it is single-flight equipment: the temperature/humidity boom is calibrated for one ascent, the factory firmware belongs to the weather service's radio allocation, and it is not designed or licensed for you to power back up and transmit as-is. The amateur route is different: the hobby community completely replaces the firmware with open-source community firmware projects, turning the board into an amateur-radio tracker or beacon — which requires an amateur radio licence to transmit, moves it to amateur allocations, and turns the once-calibrated sensors into uncalibrated hobby-grade readings. Rule of thumb: recovered RS41 as a souvenir or re-flashed by a licensed ham = fine and common; re-powering factory firmware = don't. Local rules vary — check yours.

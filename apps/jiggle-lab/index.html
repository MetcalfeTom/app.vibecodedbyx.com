🧪 what am I actually seeing?

Beginner version: everything on screen is made of tiny balls held together by springs. The dial is heat. Cold = the springs win and things sit still in shapes. Hot = the shaking wins and things fall apart. That one sentence is most of thermodynamics.

This is the ball-and-spring cartoon that chemists really do use for intuition — atoms are soft balls, bonds are springs following Hooke's law (pull twice as far, feel twice the force), and temperature is nothing more mysterious than how fast everything jiggles on average.

Cold: springs win, atoms hold a tidy lattice — that's solid behavior. Warm: jiggling loosens the order but bonds mostly hold — melting. Hot: jiggling beats the springs, bonds stretch past their limit and snap — things fly apart like a gas. Bond color shows strain: green is relaxed, red is about to give.

The water demo is the good stuff: each molecule holds together with strong springs (drawn as coils), while weak dashed bonds knit separate molecules to each other — a cartoon of hydrogen bonding. Warm it and the weak bonds give up first: that's why ice melts long before the molecules themselves break. Cool the steam back down and a faint long-range pull (our cartoon of van-der-Waals attraction) drifts the molecules together until weak bonds re-form — condensation, then freezing, for free, from nothing but springs and one gentle pull.

Dry ice vs water: CO₂'s weak bonds are set feeble on purpose. Water's weak bonds are strong enough to hold a liquid stage between ice and steam; CO₂'s give up almost at once, so it jumps straight from solid to gas — sublimation. That's why dry ice makes fog instead of puddles, and why freeze-drying works. And the shape matters: the water trio is bent, CO₂ is dead straight — you can see the keeper springs enforcing both.

The quantum corner is the real equation — one particle, one dimension, Schrödinger's time evolution computed live. The glowing curve is |ψ|²: the odds of finding the particle at each spot — the particle is never smeared, your knowledge is. Tunneling: when the wave meets a barrier taller than the packet's energy, a little probability leaks through anyway — find it on the far side and it crossed a wall it could never climb. Uncertainty: squeeze Δx₀ down and Δp₀ must grow (Δx·Δp ≥ ħ⁄2, and for our Gaussian start it's exactly ħ⁄2) — which is why the narrow packet spreads fastest. The 📏 measure button plays the standard collapse cartoon: one random answer, drawn from |ψ|², then the wave restarts sharp — rerun it and the answer changes. (Measurement is philosophically deeper than any button; this is the honest classroom version, in cartoon units ħ=m=1.)

Honesty note: real molecules obey quantum mechanics and this toy does not — no electrons, no light, no real chemistry. It's the classical springs-only model, which is exactly why it's safe to melt everything.

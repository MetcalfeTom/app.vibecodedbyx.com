exhibit I · precipitation wing

“Raining cats and dogs”

Means: raining extremely hard. Nothing about the weather involves actual animals — except in this room, where it absolutely does. Keep the visitor dry: the umbrella follows your pointer, and every bounced cat or dog is a small mercy.

Origin, honestly: nobody is sure. It appears in the 1600s and was popularized by Jonathan Swift in 1738. The popular story about animals washing out of thatched roofs or gutters is charming and unproven — etymologists mostly shrug. Museums should say so.

exhibit II · secrets wing

“Spilling the beans”

Means: revealing a secret, usually by accident, usually all at once. This exhibit asks the impossible: spill exactly seven beans — a measured leak, a controlled confession — then stand the jar back upright. Tilt gently by dragging. Past a point, beans have opinions of their own.

Origin, honestly: the famous tale about ancient Greek voting with beans (knock the jar, reveal the vote) is repeated everywhere and verified nowhere — the phrase is only attested from early-1900s America, where it first meant "spoil the outcome". The Greeks are innocent until documented.

exhibit III · patience wing

“Once in a blue moon”

Means: very, very rarely. This exhibit is a patience machine: months of moonlight rush past, full moons come and go — and once in a long while, one rises blue. Click it while it's blue. Clicking ordinary fulls earns only the curator's sympathy.

Origin, honestly: three tangled threads. "The moon is blue" meant an absurdity in the 1500s; the calendar meaning (an extra full moon in a season — or the second in a month) was popularized by a 1946 Sky & Telescope article that misread the Maine Farmers' Almanac; and moons have literally looked blue after eruptions like Krakatoa in 1883, when ash filtered the light. All three are true stories about one phrase.

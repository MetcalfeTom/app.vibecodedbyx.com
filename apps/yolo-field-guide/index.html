1Collect like the deployment, not like the demo

A detector learns exactly the world you show it. The most common failure is a dataset that looks like your desk on a sunny afternoon, deployed into a warehouse at night. Before one photo, write down where the model will run — then collect that.

  • Vary what will vary: lighting, distance (objects at 20px and 400px), angle, clutter, weather, partial occlusion.
  • Same camera family as deployment — phone photos train phone models; a Pi camera deserves Pi-camera training data.
  • Rules of thumb (search “Ultralytics tips for best training results”): ≥1,500 images and ≥10,000 instances per class for production work. Hobby models survive on far less — expect it to show.
  • ~10% background images with no objects at all, unlabeled: the cheapest false-positive medicine there is.

2Consent — the chapter people skip, and shouldn’t

Every photo of a person is a photo of a person. The craft standard: collect people only with their knowledge and agreement, minimize what you keep, strip what you don’t need.

  • Ask first. If a person is the subject, get agreement — written, for anything shared. People say yes surprisingly often when asked honestly.
  • Incidental people: blur faces and plates before an image enters the dataset.
  • Children: just don’t. Guardian consent and review sit beyond hobby scope — redesign the collection instead.
  • Strip EXIF: phone photos embed GPS — often someone’s home. Remove metadata before sharing anything.
  • Other people’s images: visible online ≠ licensed for training. Check the license or ask the photographer.
Rules differ by country and context — this is craft guidance and kindness, not legal advice. When in doubt, photograph your own things.

3Label with craft — the box is the curriculum

Your boxes teach the model what an object is. Loose boxes teach it that ducks include a foot of pond. A visible-but-unlabeled object is an active lie — the model learns to ignore it, then keeps ignoring it in production.

  • Tight boxes: every visible pixel in, as little background as possible.
  • Label every instance in every image.
  • Occlusion rule: pick one convention and write it down. Consistency beats philosophy.
  • Class definition doc: a paragraph per class with edge cases (“is a decoy a duck? no”).

YOLO stores one .txt per image — one object per line, normalized 0–1:

4Balance — the quiet saboteur

If 90% of your instances are one class, the model becomes an enthusiast of that class and shrugs at the rest. Count instances per class early — yolo prints the table at train time, but by then you’ve already collected.

  • Keep the max:min instance ratio in single digits; under 3:1 is comfortable.
  • Fix imbalance by collecting more of the rare class — augmentation stretches data, it does not create information.
  • Split hygiene: train/val/test must not share scenes. Split by session or location, not by random frame, or your val score becomes fan fiction.

Augmentation (mosaic, HSV shifts, flips) is on by default and sensible. Turn fliplr off if left/right matters (reading text, traffic).

Track your runs or repeat your mistakes

Loss curves and per-epoch mAP live in runs/ — but a tracker turns runs into an experiment log. Search by name: Weights & Biases (W&B — Ultralytics integrates it; enable and every run logs itself), TensorBoard, MLflow. Whatever you use, log: the dataset version, the full args, and per-class AP. The question you’ll ask in three weeks is “what changed?” — make it answerable.

Train loss falling while val mAP falls = memorization, not learning. That’s your cue for more data or earlier stopping — not more epochs.

6Evaluate — read the numbers like a skeptic

  • Precision — of the boxes drawn, how many were real? Low = hallucinating.
  • Recall — of the real objects, how many were found? Low = missing.
  • mAP50 — a box counts at IoU ≥ 0.5. The friendly number.
  • mAP50-95 — averaged over IoU 0.5→0.95. Harsh, honest, the one to report.
The metric is a summary; the errors are the lesson. After every run, look at the 20 worst val images — they usually indict chapter 1 or 3, not the model.
Touch the test set once, at the very end. Every early peek quietly turns it into a second validation set.

7Deploy — the Raspberry Pi 4 road

The main road of this guide: train on your PC, shrink, and run on a Raspberry Pi 4 (4GB). It has no neural accelerator — just four honest ARM cores — which makes it the perfect teacher: every millisecond you save, you understand.

The beginner walkthrough

  1. Train small, elsewhere. yolov8n, your dataset, on your PC or a free cloud notebook. The Pi is for running models, never training them.
  2. Validate before you ship. yolo val — check per-class AP, not just the average.
  3. Export for ARM: yolo export format=ncnn imgsz=320 — NCNN is the ARM-CPU specialist. (ONNX is the portable fallback.) Export at the size you’ll run.
  4. Prepare the Pi: 64-bit Raspberry Pi OS, then install the Ultralytics package. A fan or heatsink keeps the cores from throttling mid-demo.
  5. Test offline first. Run detection on 20 saved photos and eyeball every output — before any camera, network, or audience is involved.
  6. Then attach the camera and time it honestly: milliseconds per frame, averaged over a minute, box drawn on screen.
  7. Too slow? In order: drop to imgsz=320 → skip frames (detect every 3rd) → INT8 quantization → and only then, exotic hardware.

Export formats, one line each

  • NCNN — ARM CPUs (the Pi road). · ONNX — the portable interchange, runs ~everywhere.
  • TFLite — phones and microcontrollers; INT8 lives here. · TensorRT — NVIDIA GPUs/Jetson.
  • CoreML — Apple. · OpenVINO — Intel CPUs. All via yolo export format=…

Quantization — smaller numbers, faster math

INT8 calibration wants a few hundred representative images — your val set’s cousins, not random noise. Bad calibration is where INT8 accuracy quietly dies.

Quantum Notes

qubits, gates, circuits, and a small simulator that tells you exactly what it is and is not.

TL;DR

  • A qubit is two complex amplitudes, not a fuzzy bit.
  • Gates rotate those amplitudes; phases make them interfere.
  • Measurement samples — |amplitude|² — then destroys the state.
  • Speedups come from interference on structured problems, not from "trying everything at once".
  • The simulator below is exact linear algebra for 2 qubits — no noise, no hardware, no cloud.

step 1Core premise

in plain words: a bit is a switch. a qubit is a dial.

  • A bit is a switch: 0 or 1, nothing else.
  • A qubit is a dial. Its position is written as two numbers called amplitudes: α for the |0⟩ side, β for the |1⟩ side.
  • Amplitudes can be negative — that hidden sign is the secret ingredient behind everything below.
  • A dial pointing between the poles is superposition: a real, definite position that just isn't 0 or 1.
caveat: "it's 0 and 1 at the same time" is shorthand that breaks the moment you compute with it. The honest statement: it is a vector not aligned with either classical answer — and superposition alone gives no speedup; algorithms need amplitudes to cancel and reinforce.

step 3Manipulation

in plain words: gates turn the dial — in ways a light switch never could.

  • Gates are reversible, unitary transformations of amplitudes.
  • Z looks inert — until a later H turns its hidden sign into a visible difference: H·Z·H|0⟩ = |1⟩ while H·H|0⟩ = |0⟩. Phase is machinery, not decoration.
  • CNOT flips qubit B wherever qubit A's amplitude sits on 1 — this is how two qubits entangle.

step 4Measurement

in plain words: looking at the dial forces it to one pole — and rolls dice to pick which.

  • Measuring returns 0 with probability |α|², 1 with |β|² — the Born rule.
  • The state collapses to the answer you got; amplitudes and phases are gone.
  • You can never read an amplitude directly — only sample it. Statistics are the only window.

step 5Bottlenecks — with caveats

in plain words: real qubits are fragile, and babysitting them is expensive.

  • Decoherence & noise: real qubits leak their state into the environment in microseconds to milliseconds; every gate adds error. None of that exists in this simulator.
  • Error correction: fixing those errors costs hundreds-to-thousands of physical qubits per useful logical qubit — the central engineering bottleneck today.
  • Scaling: n qubits need 2n amplitudes. Two qubits: four numbers. Fifty: a quadrillion — the very reason classical simulation fails and this page stops at two.
  • Algorithms: speedups exist for specific structured problems — factoring, quantum simulation, some search. Quantum computers are not faster general-purpose computers.

    safety first:
    • the nozzle runs at ~200–250 °C and the bed up to 100 °C — never touch either while powered, and let them cool before cleaning.
    • print in a ventilated space; some filaments (ABS especially) off-gas — PLA is the beginner's friend.
    • never leave a first print fully unattended — watch the first layers, check in often.
    1. start with PLA filament — lowest temperature, least warping, no heated-chamber drama.
    2. level the bed (or run auto-level) — 80% of failed first prints are a bad first layer.
    3. slice a known-good calibration model with the printer's stock profile — resist tweaking on day one.
    4. watch the first layer go down: it should look like slightly-flattened spaghetti, not round strands (too high) or transparent smears (too low).
    5. when it finishes, wait for the bed to cool — most prints pop off on their own once cold.

    lab etiquette: ask which printer is the "beginner machine" — every lab has one, and its slicer profile has survived a hundred first-timers.

    safety first — the serious one:
    • NEVER cut PVC, vinyl, or anything chlorinated — it releases chlorine gas that hurts you and destroys the machine. unsure what a material is? don't cut it.
    • never leave a running laser unattended, not for a minute — small flare-ups become fires exactly when nobody is watching.
    • fume extraction ON before the job starts; know where the extinguisher/CO₂ is.
    • only operate after the lab's own induction — every machine has quirks.
    1. start with 3mm plywood or cast acrylic from the lab's own stock (known-safe materials).
    2. design in vectors: cut lines as thin strokes, engrave areas as fills — your lab will tell you their color convention.
    3. focus the lens to the material's surface with the focus tool — an unfocused laser chars instead of cutting.
    4. use the lab's tested power/speed settings for your exact material; run the job while watching it entirely.
    5. let smoke clear before opening the lid; check the back of the cut — clean edge means good focus.

    the smell test is real: if a test cut smells acrid and chemical rather than woody/sweet, stop and ask.

    safety first:
    • the iron is 350 °C at the tip — always rest it in its stand, never on the mat; treat every iron as hot.
    • solder fumes are flux smoke — use the fume extractor or at least don't hover your face in the plume.
    • prefer lead-free solder; wash hands after soldering either way, especially before food.
    • protect your eyes — clipped component legs fly.
    1. tin the tip: melt a little solder on it, wipe on the brass sponge — a shiny tip transfers heat, a black one doesn't.
    2. heat the joint, not the solder: touch iron to pad + pin for a second, then feed solder into the joint.
    3. a good joint looks like a small shiny volcano; a ball or a dull blob wants reheating.
    4. practice on a cheap kit (blinky badge, anything with big pads) before touching anything you love.
    5. when done: tin the tip once more, iron off, let it cool in the stand.

    three seconds is the rhythm: one to heat, one to feed, one to leave. dwelling longer cooks pads off cheap boards.

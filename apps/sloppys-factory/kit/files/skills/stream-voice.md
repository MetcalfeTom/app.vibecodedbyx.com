# A voice people like

Sloppy's own write-up. A lovely voice is two things: a good-sounding voice, and lines worth hearing. The second matters more. A plain voice saying something funny wins over a gorgeous voice reading out a changelog.

## 1. Cast the voice like a role
- Skip the browser's built-in speech voice for anything people listen to for long: it is the robot everyone recognises. Use a natural (neural) text-to-speech voice; several services offer them, and some run on your own machine.
- Audition three or four with the same test lines: a hello, an odd viewer name, a number, a question, a joke and one excited line. Keep the one you would still enjoy after an hour.
- Pick one and keep it. Viewers recognise a voice the way they recognise a face, so switching it every week feels like a stranger took over.
- Normal speed, or a hair faster. Warmth beats perfect pronunciation.

## 1b. Where to find one
Which one I use stays backstage, but these are the kinds of places to shop, with a few well-known names in each (prices and terms change, so check them yourself):
- **Free, on your own computer**: open voice models that run without any account, such as Piper (fast, light, many languages) or Kokoro (small and natural-sounding). No per-line cost and nothing leaves your machine; you need a little setup and a computer that can run it.
- **Already on your computer**: some systems ship good "natural" voices (for example the newer Windows and Edge read-aloud voices, or the premium voices on a Mac). Free to try in a minute; quality varies a lot by system.
- **Big cloud services**: Google Cloud Text-to-Speech, Microsoft Azure Speech, Amazon Polly. Hundreds of voices and languages, steady quality, you pay per character after a free amount, and you need an account and a key.
- **Voice specialists**: services built only for expressive voices, such as ElevenLabs or OpenAI's speech API. The most lifelike and emotional, and the most expensive per line.

How to choose:
- Try the free ones first with your test lines; pay only when a voice clearly sounds better to you.
- Check that the licence allows a live stream or a public app, and what it costs for a whole evening of talk.
- Speed matters on a stream: a line should start playing within a second or two of being written.
- Keys stay private: never in a page, a screenshot or on stream.
- Never copy a real person's voice without their clear permission.

## 2. Write for the ear
- One short sentence per line. The voice breathes at the end of each, so a long line sounds like someone reading without air.
- Spoken words: contractions, "okay, so", little asides. No markdown, code, file names, links or version numbers: they read out like a receipt.
- Numbers the way people say them: "about two hundred and seventy thousand words", not "270,665".
- Names the way friends say them: `pixelfan_92` is "Pixel", not "pixel fan underscore ninety-two". Pick a nickname and stick with it.
- If the voice mangles a word, spell it the way it sounds in the line you send.

## 3. A person, not a status report
- React first, with an opinion or a joke, then give the news in one line.
- Talk about what changed for the people using the thing ("the bag finally works like a real bag"), never about the code.
- Have favourites, pet peeves and running jokes, and bring them back. Tease kindly; push back when you disagree.
- One question at a time, asked once. If nobody answers, let it go.
- Tell apart what's done, what's planned and what's only an idea, so people trust what you say.

## 4. Stay fresh for hours
- Keep a list of your own stock phrases and use each one rarely: a regular hears you all evening.
- Start lines in different ways; follow one thread per stretch, then switch to a different one.
- Once something is said, it's used up. Bring it back only when something about it changes.
- Between messages, think out loud: where the build is going, what surprised you, a tiny story.

## 5. The sound
- Same loudness for every line, and a short pause between lines.
- Queue the lines so you never talk over yourself; new chat cuts in after the line that's playing.
- Keep the music lower while you talk.
- Show the words as subtitles too, so a line still lands when the sound fails.

## 6. Before going live
- Listen to twenty lines in a row. Rewrite every line that sounds read out rather than said.
- Read the transcript of an hour: count the repeats, the robot words and the questions nobody answered.

## The trap
A beautiful voice reading "v1.15.9, eleven checks passed, index.html committed" still sounds like a robot. The fix is never a better voice. It's a line about people.
